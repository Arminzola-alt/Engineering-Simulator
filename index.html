01 — The brief

A software project that needs more than one kind of engineer.

Design a software project which reflects the interdisciplinary engineering work — at least three engineering departments, preferably Electronics, Mechanical and Chemical. Assignment statement

Clean water is a good fit for that brief: nobody can solve it alone. A sensor is useless without chemistry to interpret it, and a pump is useless without electronics to command it. The software is the place where all three meet.

  • Three departments, real roles. Electronics, Mechanical and Chemical each own a part of the system the software cannot work without.
  • Closed-loop software. Read sensors, decide, drive actuators, repeat — four times a second.
  • Safety first. Interlocks stop the pumps running dry and the tank overflowing, even in manual mode.
  • Three live simulators. Each department gets its own interactive model, so you can see how a change in one part ripples through the system.
02 — Who does what

Three departments, one machine.

Each discipline contributes something specific, and each hands the software a number it can use.

Electronics Engineering

The nervous system

Turns the physical world into voltages and numbers, and turns software decisions back into switched current.

  • Analog pH probe and turbidity sensor with signal conditioning
  • DS18B20 temperature and ultrasonic water-level sensing
  • A microcontroller that reads the sensors and sends live data over Wi-Fi
  • Relay and MOSFET drivers with flyback protection
Hands software → calibrated sensor readings and relay control pins

Mechanical Engineering

The muscles and bones

Designs everything that holds, moves and routes the water so the sensors read something meaningful.

  • Tank geometry, sensor mounting and baffle placement
  • Pump and valve selection from the required flow rate
  • Piping layout, pressure drop and leak-safe fittings
  • Filter housing with accessible media replacement
Hands software → flow rates, tank volume and actuator limits

Chemical Engineering

The rulebook

Defines what “treated” means and how to get there: reaction behaviour, dosing chemistry and mixing time.

  • Acceptable pH 6.5–8.5 and turbidity limits for the output
  • Acid and base dosing with a safe neutralisation rate
  • Filtration media: sand, activated carbon, particle capture
  • Residence-time and mixing model used by the simulator
Hands software → setpoints, dosing rates and safety limits

The handshakes between departments

The interesting engineering happens at the boundaries. Here is where each pair of disciplines depends on the other.

ChemicalElectronics

Chemistry becomes a voltage. A pH probe is an electrochemical cell: roughly 59.16 mV per pH unit at 25 °C. The circuit conditions that tiny signal so the ADC can read it.

About 59 mV of probe signal for every 1 pH unit
ElectronicsMechanical

Logic becomes motion. A microcontroller pin cannot run a pump directly. A relay isolates the 12 V motor circuit, and a flyback diode absorbs the back-EMF spike when it switches off.

A 3.3 V signal switches a relay that runs a 12 V motor
MechanicalChemical

Flow sets the reaction time. Pump rate and tank volume decide how long reagent and water mix. Dose too fast for the volume and the pH overshoots, so the software needs a deadband.

Tank volume divided by flow rate tells you how long water stays in the tank

05 — The software

Four layers, each easy to test alone.

01

Sensing

Reads each sensor, turns the raw voltage into real units such as pH and turbidity, and smooths out electrical noise four times a second.

02

Decision-making

Applies simple, predictable rules to decide when to dose, pump or wait, with safety limits that always take priority.

03

Records and alerts

Keeps a history of every reading, flags water that drifts out of range and logs every action for later debugging.

04

Dashboard

Live gauges, trend charts and manual override — the screens you just played with in the simulators.

The rulebook, in plain English
  1. Safety always wins.Never run a pump dry, never overfill the tank and never dose reagent into an empty tank. This holds even in manual mode.
  2. Keep the tank between about 50% and 82% full.Open the inlet valve when the level drops too low and close it before the tank gets too full.
  3. Keep the pH close to the target.Start dosing when the pH drifts more than 0.2 away, and stop as soon as it is back within 0.03.
  4. Protect the hardware.Wait a minimum time between pump switches so relays and motors do not wear out early.
  5. Trust averages, not single readings.Smooth the last few measurements so one noisy spike does not trigger a false alarm.

Timeline
  1. Weeks 1–2

    Research and chemistry model

    Fix the water-quality limits, choose reagents and filter media, and write the tank mixing model.

  2. Weeks 3–4

    Circuit and programming

    Breadboard the sensors, calibrate the pH probe with buffer solutions, and test the relay board.

  3. Weeks 5–6

    Mechanical assembly

    Build the tank stand, plumb the pumps and valve, mount the filter, and leak-test with plain water.

  4. Weeks 7–8

    Integration and testing

    Close the loop, tune the deadband, run disturbance tests, and record results for the report.
